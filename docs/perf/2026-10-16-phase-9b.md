# Phase 9B load tests, 2026-10-16

Decision record: [ADR 0025](../adr/0025-load-tests.md). Everything ran on one laptop (MacBook M1 Pro, 8 cores,
16 GB; Docker Desktop with Kafka, Postgres 512 MB, Redis, Keycloak, Apicurio, WireMock and Grafana LGTM; Flink in an
embedded MiniCluster, parallelism 8; the platform, both MCP servers and fake-llm as local JVMs). The load generator
shares the machine, so every figure is a lower bound on what the code can do and an upper bound on what this laptop
can.

Latencies come from the database (`alert.event_time`, `detected_at`, `received_at`, `case_analysis.created_at` and
`latency_ms`) rather than from the Prometheus histograms: exact percentiles per run, per rule, with no bucket
interpolation. The dashboard shows the same stages live.

## 1. Detection latency: the 12.4 s of phase 9A

9A's alerts split by rule (same database rows):

| Rule | Alerts | p50 | p95 | max |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GEO_JUMP | 32 | 0.07 s | 0.14 s | 0.1 s |
| LARGE_AMOUNT | 23 | 0.08 s | 0.33 s | 0.6 s |
| VELOCITY | 21 | 8.1 s | 12.2 s | 14.1 s |

Not Flink catching up on the backfill (the suspicion): the simulator stamped a burst's ten transactions
`now - 20 s ... now - 2 s` and published them together, so the 8th, which tips the velocity rule, was already about 6 s
old when it was sent (older still when the account had other recent payments). The simulator now publishes each
scenario transaction at its event time (`TrafficGeneratorTest`); VELOCITY's p95 at 50 tps is 151 ms.

## 2. Stream: throughput and detection latency by step

`scripts/stack.sh traffic --tps=N --fraud-every=1 --duration=120` (90 s from 2,000 tps), sampled with
`scripts/perf/flink-rate.py`. 40,000 accounts up to 1,000 tps, 200,000 above (so ordinary traffic stays under the
velocity threshold); each step about 120 scenarios.

| tps | Alerts | Detection p50 | p95 | p99 | max | Busiest subtask (ms busy per s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 93 | 85 ms | 142 ms | 477 ms | 914 ms | 7 |
| 100 | 105 | 90 ms | 181 ms | 280 ms | 690 ms | 5 |
| 200 | 123 | 90 ms | 264 ms | 775 ms | 1.2 s | 8 |
| 500 | 134 | 137 ms | 476 ms | 2.0 s | 3.2 s | 14 |
| 1,000 | 195 | 145 ms | 392 ms | 463 ms | 623 ms | 21 |
| 2,000 | 109 | 119 ms | 332 ms | 641 ms | 747 ms | 33 |
| 5,000 | 241 | 112 ms | 254 ms | 398 ms | 532 ms | 61 |
| 10,000 | 7,034 | 209 ms | 626 ms | 864 ms | 1.2 s | 102 |

At 10,000 tps the 200,000 accounts average three payments a minute and ordinary traffic tripped the velocity rule
7,000 times (the alerts are real, the scenario is not). Above 10,000 tps the limit is the single-threaded simulator and
Kafka inside Docker, not Flink: its busiest subtask is busy a tenth of the time.

**Maximum rate, catching up on a backlog** (job stopped, 4,000,000 transactions published, job started): drained in
about 50 s, 92,000 transactions/s at the peak and about 80,000/s on average. The busiest subtask was saturated (busy
1,000 ms/s) while the JVM used 2.65 cores: the topic's 6 partitions give 6 busy source tasks (Avro decoding and the
landing-zone writer are chained into them), so more partitions, not more cores, would raise it.

**Found on the way.**

- *The job crash-looped above about 200,000 accounts.* Checkpoints were kept in the JobManager's memory (Flink's default
  without a configured storage), which allows 5 MB of state per subtask; the rule state of 200,000 accounts exceeded it,
  every checkpoint failed, the job failed and restarted from an old checkpoint, over and over. That run read p95 24.7 s
  at 5,000 tps. Checkpoints now go to a file system (`checkpoint.dir`, default `~/.finsentinel/checkpoints`); the rows
  above for 2,000 tps and more are after the fix.
- *Flink's per-second meters average over a minute.* The first catch-up looked like 8,000 transactions/s: 480,000
  read in a few seconds, averaged over 60 s. The sampler computes rates from counters and offsets instead; the web
  UI's metrics refresh every 10 s, so it samples no faster.

## 3. The platform's copy of the transactions

The platform stores every transaction in Postgres (`TransactionStoreListener`) so the agents can look up history. It is
the end-to-end ceiling, far below Flink:

| Setting | Rows/s |
|---|---|
| Default (one consumer thread, batches of 500) | 3,000-4,200 |
| `reWriteBatchedInserts=true` on the JDBC URL | 3,000-4,200 (no change) |
| `spring.kafka.listener.concurrency=3` | 4,500-5,900 |
| ... and `max-poll-records=2000` | 4,600-5,200 (no change) |
| Default, txn table at about 8 million rows | 1,300 |

Postgres's active inserts waited on `IO:DataFileRead/Write`: the table and its two indexes (one on a random text id)
no longer fit the container's 128 MB of shared buffers. At 5,000 tps it fell 460,000 transactions behind.

**Consequence: analyses ran before their evidence.** While the copy lagged, 44 cases failed: 48 of their 52 alert
transactions were stored after the alert had arrived, so `getAlertTransactions` returned nothing, fake-llm (which cites
only returned ids) wrote no findings, and the validator rejected the report after its repair. A real model would have
written a report without the transactions. Not fixed in 9B; see ADR 0025 "Next".

## 4. Platform: alert bursts and the case queue

`stack.sh traffic --burst=N` puts N fraud scenarios on N different accounts at once (most alerts arrive within a
second; a velocity burst takes 14 s to tip). Queue = alert received to analysis start; total = payment to stored
report.

**fake-llm at full speed** (analysis p95 0.1-1.7 s), 120 alerts:

| batch-size / poll-interval | Drain | Queue p50 | Queue p95 | Total p50 | Total p95 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 / 5 s (default) | 306 s | 151 s | 277 s | 152 s | 277 s |
| 8 / 1 s | 21 s | 5.9 s | 14.6 s | 6.5 s | 15.6 s |
| 32 / 1 s | 16 s | 1.8 s | 5.9 s | 3.7 s | 6.6 s |

**fake-llm at a real model's pace** (3-7 s per call, analysis p95 18-19 s against Luna's 22.9 s in ADR 0023), 40
alerts:

| batch-size / poll-interval | Drain | Queue p50 | Queue p95 | Total p50 | Total p95 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 / 5 s (default) | 427 s | 206 s | 379 s | 218 s | 394 s |
| 8 / 1 s | 115 s | 42.5 s | 68.5 s | 60.1 s | 84.8 s |
| 32 / 1 s | 40.5 s | 2.5 s | 9.1 s | 20.9 s | 25.6 s |

The default drains at most 2 cases per (5 s + the slower of the two analyses): 0.38 cases/s with an instant model,
0.1/s with a real one. A worker also waits for its whole batch before it polls again, so every round lasts as long as
its slowest analysis (head-of-line blocking): with 8 per claim and a real model's tail, the queue's p95 is still a
minute.

**An alert storm** (the 10,000 tps step): about 7,000 alerts were taken in with no visible consumer lag; 7,854 cases
were then analysed at 32 / 1 s in about 7 minutes (18-22 cases/s, fake-llm).

## 5. Budgets under load

**`max-per-hour` across two instances.** Both instances (8090 and 8097) with `max-per-hour` = the analyses already in
the last hour (481) + 30 = 511, 8 per claim, 1 s poll; a burst of 120 alerts. In the next 90 s they ran 38 analyses
(22 and 16: the 30 plus 8 that aged out of the hour meanwhile); the count in the trailing hour peaked at exactly 511,
never above; 81 cases waited; both logged "Hourly analysis budget of 511 reached". The budget is checked and claimed
under one Postgres advisory lock (ADR 0004), so instances cannot overshoot it together.

**Regulation search budget over MCP.** `McpSimulation`: 300 searches in a minute against
`max-regulation-searches-per-hour: 200`: exactly 200 answered, 100 came back as tool errors ("budget ... is used up");
the Postgres counter read 300 attempts in the window.

## 6. Analyst API and MCP endpoint (Gatling)

`AnalystApiSimulation`: each virtual user is one analyst visit (realm token, queue of 50 awaiting decision, one case's
detail with its ETag, and in 20% of visits a decision with `If-Match`); open model, 20 s ramp, 1 min hold; 8,000 cases
awaiting decision.

| Visits/s | Requests | Throughput | p50 | p95 | p99 | max | Failed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 1,541 | 19 rps | 12 ms | 26 ms | 50 ms | 292 ms | 0 |
| 25 | 3,865 | 48 rps | 8 ms | 17 ms | 43 ms | 144 ms | 0 |
| 50 | 7,731 | 97 rps | 7 ms | 14 ms | 63 ms | 261 ms | 0 |
| 100 | 15,384 | 192 rps | 6 ms | 18 ms | 108 ms | 275 ms | 0 |
| 200 | 30,817 | 385 rps | 6 ms | 37 ms | 124 ms | 372 ms | 0 |
| 500 | 58,644 | 501 rps | 15.6 s | 40.2 s | 51.6 s | 60 s | 27.6% |
| 1,000 | 88,950 | 741 rps | 15.6 s | 45.1 s | 59.7 s | 60 s | 63.3% |

From 200 visits/s on, two visits regularly picked the same case: 4,643 decisions got 409 (the case had changed since
its detail was read), which is If-Match doing its job, not an error. The knee between 385 and about 900 rps is the
**audit log**: every request appends to its bank's hash chain (ADR 0016), which locks the chain's head row; with two
banks every request queues on one of two locks while holding a pooled connection, the pool (10) runs dry ("Connection
is not available", 5,243 times, all in `AuditFilter`) and requests time out. 197 decisions failed with 500 in those
two runs only.

`McpSimulation`: 10 assistant visits/s (`listCases`, then `getCase`) for 1 min plus the searcher above: 1,720 requests,
`listCases` p95 32 ms, `getCase` p95 15 ms, 0 failed. Successful MCP reads are not audited (only refusals), so this path
does not touch the chain.

## 7. One real-model sample (gpt-6-luna)

The default profile (OpenAI), 8 per claim, 1 s poll, `max-attempts=1` and `max-per-hour` 25 above the hour's count as
hard caps; a burst of 20 scenarios gave 23 alerts.

| | |
|---|---|
| Reports | 22 of 23 (one transfer case rejected by the validator after its repair: no regulation search; not retried) |
| Cost | $0.0659 in total, $0.0030 per analysis (max $0.0049); 61 model calls |
| Analysis | p50 24.1 s, p95 38.4 s, max 41 s |
| Queue | p50 30.7 s, p95 74.4 s |
| Payment to report | p50 46.8 s, p95 107 s; all 23 done 124 s after the first alert |

| Rule | Analyses | Cost each | Analysis time |
|---|---|---|---|
| GEO_JUMP | 8 | $0.0016 | 14.4 s |
| VELOCITY | 9 | $0.0035 | 26.7 s |
| LARGE_AMOUNT (transfers) | 5 | $0.0043 | 33.5 s |

Analyses took twice the golden-case time (p50 11.6 s, ADR 0023) and cost 2.5 times as much: live cases make more tool
calls (2.8 model calls each) and ran 8 at a time against the provider. The real alert-to-report p95 of a burst is set
by the queue: at the 2-per-5-s default these 23 would have taken about 7 minutes (12 rounds of 5 s plus the slower of two analyses).
